There is a moment, familiar to every photographer, when an image leaves your hands. It existed in the viewfinder, then on the card, then in the edit. At each stage you were in control. You decided the frame, the exposure, the crop, the grade. And then it goes somewhere — a print, a book, an exhibition wall, a screen — and suddenly the context around it is doing half the work.

A photograph hung floor-to-ceiling in a white gallery tells the viewer something before they've even looked at the image. It says: stand here, take your time, this is worth your full attention. A photograph printed at postcard size in a dense grid on a studio wall says something different: there are many of these, look across them, find the connections. Neither presentation is better. But they are not interchangeable. The wrong context doesn't just fail to help the image — it actively undermines it.

This is obvious in the physical world. Nobody would hang a hundred prints salon-style and ask the viewer to contemplate each one individually. Nobody would place a single image in a vast room and expect the viewer to assess the breadth of a career from it. The space tells the viewer how to look, and the how is inseparable from the what.

On the web, we have almost entirely abandoned this thinking.

---

## The default is nothing

Most photographer websites are built on the same assumption: here is a container, put your images in it, choose a layout. Grid or slideshow. Sidebar or no sidebar. Dark background or light. These are presented as design decisions, but they are not. They are aesthetic preferences detached from any reasoning about the work itself.

The result is that almost every photographer's website behaves the same way regardless of what the photography is doing. A photojournalist working on long-term documentary projects and a commercial food photographer end up with structurally identical sites. The only difference is the images inside them. And this means the site is doing nothing — it is a neutral container at best, and at worst it is quietly working against the photographer by presenting the work in a context that doesn't understand it.

This is not a criticism of photographers. It is a failure of the tools available. When every template offers the same set of options, the message is clear: the structure doesn't matter, just pick what looks nice. But structure does matter. It has always mattered. The reason a photography book has a designer is not to make it pretty — it is to make decisions about sequence, pacing, scale, and rhythm that serve the specific images inside it. A website deserves the same consideration, but it almost never gets it.

---

## What does the viewer need?

Before any decision about layout, navigation, typography, or interaction, there is a question that almost never gets asked: what is the person visiting this site supposed to do when they get there?

This sounds simple but it isn't, because the answer is different for different photographers — and not different in the way you might expect. It has very little to do with what you photograph. It has everything to do with why someone is looking at it.

Consider three scenarios.

A person arrives at your site because they encountered one of your images somewhere — a feature, a social post, a recommendation — and they want to see more of your work. They are there for the experience of looking. They want to be moved, provoked, impressed, or transported by individual images. They are not in a hurry. They are not comparing you to anyone. They do not need to know your rates or your client list. They need to see your work in a context that lets each image do what it was made to do.

A different person arrives at your site because they saw a project of yours and want to understand it more fully. Maybe it was a series — twenty images from a place, a community, a period of time. They are interested not in a single image but in the body of work. They want to move through it in order. They may want context — a few words about why this project exists, where it was made, what it means to you. They are engaging with the narrative, not just the individual frames.

A third person arrives at your site because they are considering hiring you. They may be an art director, a brand manager, an editor, a couple planning a wedding. They need to assess your capability and range quickly. They want to see enough work across enough categories to feel confident that you can deliver what they need. They also want to know how to contact you, what your process is, perhaps who else you've worked for. They are making a professional evaluation, and the site needs to support that efficiently.

These three visitors need fundamentally different things. Not slightly different. Fundamentally. And the architecture of the site — not the colour scheme, not the font, not the logo placement, but the actual bones of how it is structured — should be built entirely around which of these visitors you are serving.

---

## Architecture follows intent

In the first scenario, the architecture is subtractive. Almost everything that typically exists on a website is unnecessary and actively harmful to the experience. Navigation should be minimal or invisible. There should be no grid of thumbnails competing for attention. Each image should occupy as much of the screen as possible, presented one at a time, with enough space around it to let the eye settle. The viewer's journey is sequential — one image, then the next — and the pace is set by them, not by the site. Text is sparse. Maybe a title. Maybe a year. The image carries all the meaning and the site's only job is to not get in the way.

This sounds easy but it is technically and philosophically demanding. It means the site must handle every aspect ratio gracefully — a vertical portrait and a horizontal landscape must both feel considered in the same viewing context. It means transitions between images matter because the gap between one photograph and the next is a moment of recalibration for the viewer, just as it is when turning the page of a book. It means the site cannot have a busy header or a persistent menu or a cookie banner that sits over the image, because all of these things break the contract with the viewer, which is: right now, there is only this photograph.

In the second scenario, the architecture is narrative. The primary unit is not the image but the project. The site's home or index page needs to present projects as distinct bodies of work, each with enough identity to draw the viewer in — but not so much that the index becomes the experience. A single image per project, a title, perhaps a date or a brief line of context. When the viewer enters a project, the images are sequenced deliberately. This is not a gallery where order is irrelevant — it is a story where the first image sets a tone, the middle images develop it, and the final image resolves or opens something. Text may be woven between images, not as captions but as narrative. An introduction might set the scene. A mid-point text might shift the viewer's understanding. The architecture supports this interplay between image and language, and treats both as first-class elements of the work.

This is the mode closest to a photography book, and it carries similar responsibilities. The sequence must be respected — the viewer should not be able to easily randomise or shortcut through it without understanding that they are skipping ahead in a story. At the same time, the site should not be so rigid that it feels like a forced march. The balance is between authorial control and viewer comfort, and the architecture must negotiate this carefully.

In the third scenario, the architecture is functional. The viewer is making a decision, and the site needs to support that decision with clarity and efficiency. This means categorisation matters — the work should be organised in a way that lets the viewer quickly find what is relevant to them. If they are looking for portrait work, they should not have to scroll through landscape and editorial to find it. The density of images is higher here, not because the images matter less but because the viewer's purpose is different. They are scanning, comparing, assessing. A grid layout makes sense because it allows for rapid visual evaluation. Project pages are shorter — six to twelve images rather than thirty — because the viewer needs to see enough to be convinced but not so much that they lose focus. Contact information is prominent, not because it is more important than the work but because without it the site fails its fundamental purpose. An about page carries weight here. A client list, press mentions, testimonials — these are not vanity but evidence, and the viewer is looking for evidence.

## Why the genre is irrelevant

It would be tempting to map these architectures to genres. Landscape and fine art in the first. Documentary and editorial in the second. Commercial and wedding in the third. But this would be wrong, and it is exactly the mistake that most template makers and most photographers themselves fall into.

A landscape photographer whose work is exhibited in galleries and collected by museums has a fundamentally different presentation need than a landscape photographer who licenses images to tourism boards. They both photograph landscapes. Their sites should have almost nothing in common.

A wedding photographer who approaches their work as long-form documentary — spending months with a couple, producing a body of work that tells the story of a relationship — needs a narrative architecture. A wedding photographer who needs to attract new bookings through their site needs a functional one. They are in the same genre. They need different structures.

Even a single photographer may find that their work spans these categories. Many photographers have a personal practice that is project-based and exploratory, and a commercial practice that requires client acquisition. This is not a problem to solve with one compromise site that tries to do everything. It is a recognition that these are genuinely different functions and might warrant different approaches.

The point is that the question is never "what do you photograph" but rather "what is this site for." And the answer to that question determines everything — not as a matter of preference but as a matter of logic.

---

## The decisions that have already been made

If you accept the reasoning above — that the structure of a photographer's website should follow directly from the purpose it serves — then something interesting happens. Most of the decisions you thought you needed to make have already been made for you.

Should you have a thumbnail grid on your landing page? That depends entirely on whether your site exists to let people contemplate individual images, to present narrative projects, or to demonstrate professional range. In one case the answer is no, absolutely not. In another it is yes, necessarily. The decision is not about what looks good. It is about what is logically consistent with the site's purpose.

Should you have an about page? If your site is a space for experiencing images, an about page is a distraction — the viewer does not need to know about you to experience the work. If your site is a professional tool, the about page is essential — it is part of the evidence the viewer is evaluating.

Should your images be presented large or small, one at a time or many at once, with text or without? These are not style questions. They are structural consequences of intent.

This is what it means for a website to be finished — not that the code works and the pages load, but that every element exists for a reason and no element is left to chance or preference. The photographer using such a site does not need to decide between five layout options because the correct layout has already been determined by the logic of what the site is for.

---

## The invitation

There is a particular kind of relief in being told that a decision has been made well and you don't need to revisit it. Photographers experience this when working with a good book designer — the designer's job is not to offer options but to understand the work deeply enough to make the right choices and defend them. The photographer can then focus on what they are actually good at, which is the images.

A website should work the same way. Not as a set of possibilities but as a resolved proposition. The architecture, the navigation, the typography, the spacing, the way images are loaded and presented and transitioned between — all of these should reflect a single, coherent line of thinking about what this particular site is for and how it should behave.

The question, then, is not which template looks best. It is a more honest and more useful question: what is the purpose of your site, and does its structure serve that purpose completely?

If you can answer the first part, the second part should already be solved.
